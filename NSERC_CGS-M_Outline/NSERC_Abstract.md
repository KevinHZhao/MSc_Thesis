In studies on gene association, two major forms of observational study design have emerged: the case-control study, and the case-parents study.  While the case-control study is the more common of the two, it suffers under population stratification.  The case-parents design, where each "case" child is given two "controls" in their parents, remedies this.  From case-parent data, we may assess not only the risk a child's genes may bring upon themselves, but also the risk a mother's genes may bring upon their child (such as during fetal development).  However, several other factors involving gene interactions can further complicate an analysis.  This includes mating asymmetry, where mothers and fathers in a population have different distributions of genetic factors, gene-environment effects, where a gene only worsens the risk produced by some environmental factor such as smoking, and imprinting effects, where a child's risk from having a genetic factor is dependent on that factor's parent of origin.  Furthermore, when obtaining one's genetic information, if the child has two different factors of the same gene, we do not always know which factor came from which parent.  Hence analyzing the effects of imprinting requires us to solve a missing value problem in data.  Although there are so many factors to be considered, these effects may be assessed simultaneously via a log-linear model.  We may also impute missing data for imprinting via an Expectation-Maximization algorithm.  While this process has been outlined before, no software is presently available for researchers to efficiently perform this analysis.  For our research project, we will create a freely available R package allowing anyone to model these genetic effects and obtain statistically sound conclusions.
