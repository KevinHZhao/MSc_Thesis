\chapter{Simulation study}\label{chap:simstudy}

In this chapter, we use \textbf{TriLLIEM} to conduct several simulation studies.
First, we validate the log-linear model's performance by simulating and analyzing data under a large variety of conditions using \textbf{TriLLIEM}, evaluating model power and Type 1 error rate.
We then perform a similar study on data simulated with population stratification to verify whether the log-linear model produces spurious gene-environment interactions, with a focus on imprinting by environment interactions.

\section{Model validation simulations}

\subsection{Data simulation conditions}

Having shown that \textbf{TriLLIEM} is consistent with \textbf{EMIM} and \textbf{Haplin} in \cref{sec:compare}, we utilize its ability to easily simulate and analyze data to validate the log-linear model's performance.
We focus on the performance of \textbf{TriLLIEM}'s imprinting model, since it is more flexible than Haplin's.
Another major point of interest is the performance of the non-stratified gene-environment interaction model, which is not available in \textbf{EMIM} or \textbf{Haplin}.
With these considerations in mind, we simulated data under $72$ distinct conditions, summarized in \cref{tab:summcond}.

For each condition, we simulated $2,000$ data sets with $2,000$ case trios to test the case-triad model, and another $2,000$ data sets with $1,000$ case and $1,000$ control trios to test the hybrid model.
In every simulation, we set the minor allele frequency to be $0.3$.
We considered several different cases for the mating asymmetry coefficients, $C_1$, $C_2$, and $C_4$ (see \cref{tab:bourgey}), to compare model performance under varying mating asymmetry levels.
We did not simulate true maternal effects ($S \neq 1$) or maternal gene-environment interactions, as the performance of the log-linear model under these conditions has been previously examined by Hudson \cite{Huds19}.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

We analyzed each data set using \textbf{TriLLIEM} under the HWE, MS, and MaS (when controls were available) mating type models.
For the effects included in the model, we always had $\CC$ and $\MM$, as well as the appropriate environmental interaction term (unless the model did not consider environmental exposures).
$I_{\MM}$ or $I_{\FF}$ was included when the model conditions set $I_{\MM} \not = 1$ or $I_{\FF} \not = 1$, respectively, \emph{or} if the underlying scenario use it as the environmental interaction term.
Note that this means we do not consider cases where the model was improperly defined (e.g., using maternal imprinting by environment interactions when the data was simulated with paternal imprinting by environment interactions), as the purpose of this study is to validate the model's accuracy when correctly defined.
We also used a model that did not consider the environmental variable, a model that used the single log-linear model based on the Weinberg approach (which will be referred to as the non-stratified model) and the stratified model based on the Haplin approach.
However, to fit the stratified model in \textbf{TriLLIEM}, we do not need to run the model twice, as with \textbf{EMIM} and \textbf{Haplin}.
Instead, we use the equivalent approach of including an interaction of the environmental variable with all main effects, regardless of whether the interaction is truly of interest.
For example, one of our simulation scenarios had $R=S=I_{\MM}=I_{\FF}=1$, $C_1 = C_2 = C_4 = 1.15$, an environmental factor proportion of $0.5$, and a maternal imprinting by environment interaction of $V = 1.6$.
To analyze the case-triad and hybrid data for this scenario, for the stratified approach we must include $\CC$, $\MM$, $I_{\MM}$, $\EE:\CC$, $\EE:\MM$, and $\EE:I_{\MM}$ as terms in the model whereas in \textbf{TriLLIEM} we can fit the unstratified true model with terms $\CC, \MM, I_{\MM},$ and $\EE:I_{\MM}$.
For this example, the model that does not consider the environmental variable would only use the terms $\CC, \MM,$ and $I_{\MM}$, though the results in this case would not be very useful since the data were simulated with a true maternal imprinting by environment interaction which cannot be captured by this model.

From the analysis of each simulated data set, we return the bias of each effect estimate and the result of the significance test on the appropriate terms.
We calculate the relative bias for each term in the model using the equation
\begin{equation}
  \text{Relative Bias} = \frac{\hat\beta - \beta}{\beta},
\end{equation}
where $\hat\beta$ is the estimated risk parameter, and $\beta$ is the true value of the risk parameter used to simulate the data.
We averaged the relative bias of each term in the model across all $2,000$ simulated data sets, and we determined the proportion of the $2,000$ data sets where the hypothesis test was rejected for each parameter at the $0.05$ significance level.

\subsection{Model Validation}

In this section, we summarize major observations from the results of our simulation study.
Since we ran $72$ different scenarios, it is not possible to include detailed results for all scenarios in this thesis.
Supplementary results for models with maternal imprinting, environmental variable frequency $30\%$, and MaS coefficients either $0.85$ or $1$ are given in \cref{tab:bias_eim_noc,tab:reject_eim_noc,tab:bias_eim_c,tab:reject_eim_c} in the Appendix.
To view the complete results, please visit our \href{https://github.com/KevinHZhao/MSc_Thesis/tree/main/UOthesis/tables/fulltables}{GitHub page} to download and view the relevant spreadsheets.

\subsubsection{Mating type model selection}

The presence of mating asymmetry in the simulated population has a major impact on the model's performance when we do not use the mating asymmetry model.
In our simulations with $C_1 = C_2 = C_4 = 0.85$, relative bias for the maternal effect, $\MM$, when the mating type model assumed HWE or MS was observed to always be between $-0.22$ and $-0.2$ for the case-triad model, and between $-0.13$ and $-0.11$ when control-triads were present (see, for example, the rows with ID 1--4 in \cref{tab:bias_eim_noc,tab:bias_eim_c}).
Similar results, but with bias in the opposite direction, were observed for the $1.15$ MaS coefficient scenarios; these results are available on GitHub.
This bias is due to the unaccounted mating asymmetry; when analyzing the data under the correct MaS mating type model, the largest observed absolute relative bias was approximately $0.6\%$, which is in the same range as the estimated bias for when the data were simulated without mating asymmetry and either HWE or MS was assumed.
The deviation from the true value is also evident from the higher false rejection rates for $\MM$.
When the mating asymmetry parameters are not $1$, but data are analyzed assuming a HWE or MS mating type model, the false rejection rates were always $> 15\%$, far exceeding the nomial Type 1 error rate of $5\%$ (see, for example, the rows with ID 1--4 in \cref{tab:reject_eim_noc,tab:reject_eim_c}).

Not accounting for mating asymmetry when it is present in the population appears to always bias estimates of $\MM$, but it also appears to further impact models for imprinting by environment interactions when controls are present.
For example, with data simulated with no true effects and an environmental variable frequency of $30\%$, the results of models with $\CC, \MM, I_{\MM}, \EE:I_{\MM}$ terms in the model also have inflated rejection rates for $\CC$ and $I_{\MM}$ when there are control-triads and the HWE or MS mating type models are assumed (alongside the inflated rates for $\MM$ that we previously discussed).
The rejection rates for the non-stratified model are shown in \cref{fig:reject_mas_mimpE}.
Estimated Type 1 error rates assuming the HWE and MS mating type models are higher than $18\%$ for data with simulated mating asymmetry.
The inflated rates for $\CC$ and $I_{\MM}$ are less pronounced when analyzing the respective data with mating asymmetry parameters of $1$, as the false rejection rates are less than $8\%$ for both.
This Type 1 error rate is still higher than the nominal rate of $5\%$, which could be due to having too many parameters in the model, or due to the correlation between $\CC$ and $I_{\MM}$ effects, which univariate Wald-based tests do not account for.
With only case-triad data, we no longer see the large inflation when mating asymmetry is present, despite the model's genetic effects being the same.

Interestingly, when interchanging maternal imprinting with paternal imprinting, the inflated rejection rates for $I_{\FF}$ remain, but $\CC$'s rejection rate now remains less than $8\%$ (\cref{fig:reject_mas_fimpE}).
This exception only occurs when controls are present and a HWE or MS model is assumed and child effects are only affected when maternal imprinting is included in the model rather than paternal.
A hypothesis for why this occurs could be because $I_{\MM}$ is highly dependent on both $\CC$ and $\MM$ (as the mother and child both having the minor allele is necessary for a maternal imprinting effect to be present), whereas $I_{\FF}$ is only correlated with $\CC$.
Since our Wald based p-values do not take these correlations into account, it could result in incorrect behaviour when examining the p-values of correlated effects.
This is a pattern across other similar conditions of our data when comparing imprinting models.

We can conclude from these results that mating asymmetry does not just impact the model's performance in detecting maternal effects, which is well-known \cite{Bour+11}, but can also influence the estimation of other genetic effects depending on whether control-trios are sampled and environmental interactions are in the model.

\subsubsection{Stratified vs.\ non-stratified environmental interactions}

Our simulations with environmental interactions were run using both the stratified and non-stratified approaches.
We compare the two approaches' Type 1 error and power when detecting parent-of-origin by environment interactions.
The results for the maternal imprinting by environment and paternal imprinting by environment models are shown in \cref{fig:stratcomp_Im,fig:stratcomp_If}, respectively.

Data was simulated with other genetic risks set to $1$, no mating asymmetry, and analyzed using a HWE mating asymmetry model with $\CC, \MM, I_{\MM}, \EE:I_{\MM}$ as genetic effects (alongside $\EE:\CC, \EE:\MM$ under the stratified model).  The red line represents the desired Type 1 error rate of $5\%$ when there are no true effects.\label{fig:stratcomp_Im}}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/stratcomp_Im.pdf}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}
  \caption[Comparison of average rejection rates between the stratified and non-stratified methods for $\EE:I_{\FF}$ models.]{Comparison between rejection rates and relative biases of the non-stratified (unhatched) and stratified (hatched) models for paternal imprinting by environment interactions.  Data was simulated with other genetic risks set to $1$, no mating asymmetry, and analyzed using a HWE mating asymmetry model with $\CC, \MM, I_{\FF}, \EE:I_{\FF}$ as genetic effects (alongside $\EE:\CC, \EE:\FF$ under the stratified model).  The red line represents the desired Type 1 error rate of $5\%$ when there are no true effects.\label{fig:stratcomp_If}}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/stratcomp_If.pdf}
\end{figure}

From these results, we see that the relative bias for the interaction effect, $V$, under maternal/paternal imprinting by environment interactions is low in both stratified and unstratified models, not exceeding $1.5\%$.
The Type 1 error rate is also low in both approaches, with it not exceeding $7\%$ in the stratified approach and $6\%$ in the unstratified approach.
However, when examining power (i.e.\ true effect $1.5$ or $1.6$), we find that the unstratified model has substantially higher power than the stratified model.
For example, with $2,000$ case-triads simulated with $V = 1.5$, a $30\%$ environmental variable frequency rate, and a maternal imprinting by environment interaction, the unstratified model had a power of $88\%$, while the stratified model had a power of $35\%$.
We find similar observations with $1,000$ case-triads and control-triads under the same conditions, with a power of $69\%$ and $26\%$ for the unstratified and stratified models, respectively (the reduction in power is expected, as the number of informative case-triads is lower).
This highlights a major advantage of \textbf{TriLLIEM}; it enables users to easily use the unstratified approach, which we show has better power than the stratified approach when modelling specific parent-of-origin by environment interactions.

Haplin uses a modified version of the stratified approach when modelling parent-of-origin effects, which has better power than the basic stratified approach \cite{Gjer+18}.
However, it does not allow users to test $\EE:I_{\MM}$ or $\EE:I_{\FF}$ interactions individually, instead comparing the ratios of maternal and paternal imprinting in the $\EE$ strata.
Child effects also cannot be tested for simultaneously, as this approach requires $I_{\MM}$ and $I_{\FF}$ to both be present.
This highlights another advantage of the unstratified approach, which is its flexibility.

While testing for environmental interactions, we may also concurrently include other genetic effects in the model and perform Wald tests.
For example, we can test for child effects using a non-stratified model with maternal imprinting by environment interactions (using $\CC, \MM, I_{\MM}, \EE:I_{\MM}$ as effects), the results of which we show in \cref{fig:stratcomp_C}.
We see that the relative error for $\CC$ in this approach is low, never exceeding $0.3\%$.
In significance tests, we find that the Type 1 error rate is between $5\%$ and $8\%$, which slightly exceeds our desired Type 1 error rate of $5\%$.
For the power, we observe that for data simulated with $R = 1.15$, power is roughly $47\%$ when the environmental variable frequency is $30\%$, and $40\%$ when the frequency is $50\%$.
While certainly not ideal, results such as these could be informative towards model selection.
These Type 1 and Type 2 error rates can be improved by removing non-significant terms from the model, such as the $\EE:I_{\MM}$ interaction or the $I_{\MM}$ term when appropriate.
This can be done via a likelihood ratio test, which is not currently implemented in \textbf{TriLLIEM}, though it is planned as future work.

\subsubsection{Imprinting effects}

Examining the ability of the log-linear model to correctly identify imprinting effects, we find that the model's power for detecting these effects is fair.
Similar to our previous assessment for child effects, we display results for modelling true imprinting effects when an imprinting by environment interaction effect is included in the model in \cref{fig:reject_imp}.
Relative bias is low, being consistently $<1\%$, and power increases as the magnitude of $I_{\MM}$'s true value increases, with case-triad data with $30\%$ environmental variable frequency having a power of $36\%$ when $I_{\MM} = 1.2$ and $79\%$ when $I_{\MM} = 1.4$.
From the Type 1 error rates, we see that at a $30\%$ environmental variable frequency, the false rejection rate for $I_{\MM}$ is $9\%$ with $2,000$ case-triads and $8\%$ with $1,000$ case-triads and $1,000$ control-triads.
This false rejection rate is concerning, though again, it can be reduced by removing non-significant terms from the model, such as $\CC$ or $\MM$ when applicable.

The power shown in \cref{fig:reject_imp} is actually slightly higher in the model with the added $\EE:I_{\MM}$ parameter when using data with $30\%$ environmental variable frequency, which is unexpected since a model with fewer parameters should have more power, though this could be due to simulation error.

\section{Population stratification simulation}\label{sec:popstrat}

In another simulation study, we examined the confounding effects of population stratification on the estimation of gene-environment interactions.
We simulated a population in which there were $2$ hidden sub-populations.
These sub-populations had several differing demographic parameters, which we specify in \cref{tab:subpop}.
For our population stratification simulations, we use the notation $\MAF_{i}$ for minor allele frequency, $\varepsilon_{i, 1}$ and $\varepsilon_{i, 0}$ for proportion of cases ($1$) or control ($0$) trios with the environmental variable, respectively, $\alpha_i$ for disease prevalence, and $\omega_i$ for proportion of the entire population from the $i$th sub-population (or equivalently, probability of sampling an individual from this sub-population).
%%
\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Parameters across the two sub-populations in our simulation study on population stratification.\label{tab:subpop}}
\begin{tabular}{llllll}
  \hline
  Parameter        & $\MAF_{i}$   & $\varepsilon_{i, 1}$ & $\varepsilon_{i, 0}$ & $\alpha_i$ & $\omega_i$ \\ \hline
Sub-population 1 (i = 1)  & $0.2$ & $0.3$        & $0.3$        & $0.1$    & $0.4$    \\
Sub-population 2 (i = 2) & $0.4$ & $0.5$        & $0.5$        & $0.2$    & $0.6$    \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
%%

A total of $1,000$ case and $1,000$ control trios are simulated in each data set.
To determine the proportion of case/control trios having the environmental variable, we assumed that disease status, $D$, was conditionally independent of the environmental variable, $\EE$, given population status.
Since there are no true genetic effects in this simulation study, this is a reasonable assumption, and it allows us to compute the probability of an exposed case in sub-population $X = i$ in \cref{eq:popstratex} as
\begin{align}\label{eq:popstratex}
  \Pr(X = i, \EE = 1 \mid D = 1) &= \frac{\Pr(D = 1, E = 1 \mid X = i) \Pr(X = i)}{\Pr(D = 1)} \nonumber\\
                               &= \frac{\Pr(D = 1 \mid X = i) \Pr(E = 1 \mid X = i) \Pr(X = i)}{\Pr(D = 1)} \nonumber\\
                               &= \frac{\alpha_i \varepsilon_{i, 1} \omega_i}{\sum_{j = 1}^2 \alpha_j \omega_j }.
\end{align}
Likewise, we repeat for the other combinations of $D$ and $E$ in \cref{eq:popstrat} to obtain
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:popstrat}
\begin{align}
  \Pr(X = i, \EE = 0 \mid D = 1) &= \frac{\alpha_i (1 - \varepsilon_{i, 1}) \omega_i}{\sum_{j = 1}^2 \alpha_j \omega_j }, \\
  \Pr(X = i, \EE = 1 \mid D = 0) &= \frac{(1 - \alpha_i) \varepsilon_{i, 0} \omega_i}{\sum_{j = 1}^2 (1 - \alpha_j) \omega_j }, \\   
  \Pr(X = i, \EE = 0 \mid D = 0) &= \frac{(1 - \alpha_i) (1 - \varepsilon_{i, 0}) \omega_i}{\sum_{j = 1}^2 (1 - \alpha_j) \omega_j }.\end{align}
\end{subequations}

In each sub-population, we assumed the minor allele did not affect one's disease risk (i.e. all effects were $1$), set all mating asymmetry parameters to $1$, and assumed HWE within each sub-population and Mendelian inheritance (\cref{tab:mt,tab:Mendel}).
We sampled $1,000$ case trios, assuming a multinomial distribution with probabilities in \cref{eq:popstrat,eq:popstratex} to get the number of cases from sub-population $1$ with $\EE = 1$, population $1$ with $\EE = 0$, etc., and repeated this for the controls.
Then within each of these $8$ groups, we simulated the number of trios in each genotype category, again from a multinomial distribution with probabilities from $\Pr(\MM,\FF,\CC|\DD,\EE,X=i) = \Pr(\MM,\FF|X=i) \cdot \Pr(\CC|\MM,\FF)$, and then combined the counts in each genotype category from the two sub-populations to form our final data set.
$2,000$ data sets were simulated in this manner.
We then ran \textbf{TriLLIEM} using the maternal environment interaction model and both imprinting by environment interaction models, with $\CC$ and $\MM$ always being included.
This was repeated assuming the HWE, MS, and MaS mating type models.
With this setup, we can examine how hidden population substructures affect the log-linear model's performance when environmental interactions and control trios are present.

The results with both case and control trios are shown in \cref{tab:bias_PS_c,tab:reject_PS_c}.
From these tables, we see that, as expected, population stratification does indeed lead to an increase in Type 1 error for $\CC$ and $\MM$ across all environmental interaction models, as well as larger relative biases in the range of $6\%$--$22\%$.
For instance, the false positive rates for $\CC$ and $\MM$ are greater than $20\%$ for nearly all models, except $\CC$ in a paternal imprinting by environmental effect model, where instead, the paternal imprinting term is falsely significant in over $20\%$ of the simulations.
However, examining the environmental interaction terms, we see that the false rejection rates do not exceed $7\%$, and the relative bias is roughly $4\%$.
This indicates that even under population stratification, the estimates and significance tests for environmental interactions are still valid, even though other genetic effects are inflated.

We repeated this procedure but with only $2,000$ case trios and no control trios to verify that the case trio model is indeed robust to population stratification.
We show the results of the case-only model in \cref{tab:bias_PS_noc,tab:reject_PS_noc}.
As expected, with only cases, for the main effects, the relative bias shrinks to $<0.5\%$ and the rejection rate is consistently near $0.05$.
In addition, the interaction effects are also estimated with little bias and an appropriate rejection rate.
